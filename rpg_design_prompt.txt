# Hearthfall — Design Test (phase 1)

## Goal

Prove on paper that a mobile, portrait, one-hero-plus-companions action RPG can
carry a world that is rich and deep yet simple to hold in the head, the way
Middle-earth is: a few peoples, a few powers, a history in three strokes, and
a map anyone can draw from memory. The player begins as nobody in that world
and, through battles, skill and exploration, becomes a force the world reacts
to. The game does not hand-write its adventures: an adventure engine draws on
the lore and the living-world systems to generate quests, adventures and
events that read as if they were authored. Write a design bible set, judge
it, iterate. No code, no art, no engine.

Play references: Daggerfall (a vast open land, guilds and reputations, quests
generated from the world's state, a hero who starts as no one) and the
Wizardry line (disciplined classes, dangerous dungeons, a party built from the
people you meet, death that means something). Tolkien shapes the world's
weight and clarity. All three shape the feel; none lends a name.

In scope: an original world and its first region, the living-world systems,
the adventure engine, the class system with a subclass roadmap, real-time
combat with optional autobattle, free-to-play progression and economy without
gacha, the mobile session, and a first playable region specified to the point
that a team could build it. Out of scope: engine choice, art direction, audio,
multiplayer, later regions beyond a sketch, marketing.

## The bar (non-negotiable)

- An original setting. Tolkien, Bethesda, Sir-Tech and Paizo shape the feel;
  not one name, place, god, race or artefact comes from any of them.
- Rich but comprehensible. The whole world fits on one page a new player can
  read in five minutes and then explain to a friend: at most four peoples, at
  most five powers with agendas, a history in three ages each remembered for
  one thing, a map with one shape. Depth comes from how those few things
  interlock and how far they can be explored, never from adding more of them.
- A living world, all four at once: people with schedules and memory; factions
  that act, expand, clash and change the map without the player; world time
  with days, weather, seasons and festivals that change who is where and what
  is possible; choices that close doors, alter regions and are remembered for
  the whole campaign.
- From nobody to a force. The hero starts as an ordinary person with a trade,
  a village and no destiny. Standing is earned in play and measured by the
  world: who knows the name, who asks for help, who fears it, which doors open,
  which powers court or oppose the hero. By the end of the first region the
  hero can tilt one conflict; by the end of the campaign the hero can change
  the map. There is no chosen one and no prophecy.
- An adventure engine, not a quest list. Quests, adventures and events are
  generated from the lore (peoples, powers, places, history, rumours), the
  living-world state (who is where, what factions are doing, what season it
  is) and the hero's standing and history. Generated content must read as
  authored: named people with reasons, stakes that connect to the map, and
  consequences that feed back into the simulation. Hand-written content is
  allowed only as anchors the engine builds around.
- Three classes at the start, Fighter, Mage and Rogue, each a clear discipline
  with its own verbs, risks and way of solving problems out of combat. A
  subclass roadmap of two to three subclasses per class for later phases,
  sketched so the base classes leave room for them.
- One hero under direct control, with companions met in the world who have
  their own lives, opinions and fights. Autobattle is a first-class way to
  play, never a worse one: it plays the same abilities on the same hero and
  companions, it wins the fights a competent hand would win, and hands-on play
  earns only expression and edge cases, never mandatory efficiency.
- Dungeons that matter, in the Wizardry sense: places with a reason to exist,
  layers of danger, resources that run out, and a cost to failure that the
  world remembers.
- Free to play with no gacha and no energy gate. Companions, power and story
  are earned in play. Money buys cosmetics, expansions and convenience that a
  patient player gets anyway. The design must say what is sold and prove the
  world stays credible with it.
- A phone in one hand, portrait, sessions of three to fifteen minutes that
  each end with something changed in the world, while a long evening of play
  is also rewarded.
- Never a design smell (checklist below fails a round automatically).

## Platform and business (decided)

- Mobile, portrait, one-hand play with a thumb zone; landscape never assumed.
- Real-time action: the hero moves, dodges and uses abilities directly;
  companions act on their own AI with orders from the player. Autobattle hands
  the hero to the same AI. Field fights last 20 to 90 seconds; dungeon and set
  piece fights longer.
- Free to play, no gacha, no stamina. Offline-safe: the world simulation
  advances on return, summarised in a "while you were away" page.
- Tone: a mythic land told plainly. Landscape and history as characters,
  understated magic with a price, powers with teeth, a hero who earns every
  inch of standing.

## How to work

Work in `rpg/` of this repository. Docs only. Use plain Markdown.

1. **PITCH.md first.** One page: the world in three sentences, the hero's
   start and the road to becoming a force, the loop of a session, the loop of
   a campaign, how adventures are generated, what is sold, the three things
   that make this unlike its references.
2. **The bible set.** Write these files, each standing alone and cross-linked:
   - `rpg/world/WORLD.md` — the one-page world first, verbatim as a player
     would read it; then the depth behind it: geography with a map described
     in words (regions, roads, rivers, borders and why they are where they
     are), three ages and what each left behind, the peoples, the powers and
     their agendas, magic and its price, calendar and seasons, the first region
     placed in all of this. Everything in the depth must trace back to a line
     on the one page.
   - `rpg/systems/LIVING_WORLD.md` — NPC schedules and memory (what is
     remembered, for how long, how it surfaces in dialogue, price and access),
     the faction simulation (goals, resources, moves per world-day, how
     conflicts resolve, how the map changes, what the hero can tilt), time and
     seasons (what a day, a week and a season change), consequence (how a
     choice is recorded, propagated and shown later). Every system has inputs,
     state, a tick, outputs and a worked example.
   - `rpg/systems/ADVENTURE_ENGINE.md` — how quests, adventures and events are
     generated: the inputs it reads (lore tables, world state, hero standing
     and history, companion agendas), the grammar of a quest (giver, reason,
     stake, place, opposition, twist, consequence), how names, motives and
     places are drawn from the lore so nothing reads generic, how stakes scale
     with standing (from a lost goat to a border war), how generated content
     feeds back into the simulation, how hand-written anchors are woven in,
     and how repetition is detected and avoided. Include twelve generated
     quests written out in full as the engine would produce them at four
     standings, with the inputs that produced each shown alongside.
   - `rpg/systems/CLASSES_AND_STANDING.md` — Fighter, Mage and Rogue: the
     fantasy, the verbs in and out of combat, growth from level one to the
     campaign's end, what each class cannot do; the subclass roadmap; the
     standing system (how it is earned, measured, lost, and shown), with the
     road from villager to force traced at five milestones.
   - `rpg/systems/COMBAT.md` — the hero's verbs per class, companion roles and
     AI, ability and enemy design rules, dungeon design rules, the autobattle
     policy in detail, the parity proof, difficulty, death and its cost, three
     sample encounters played twice each, by hand and on autobattle, with the
     outcome.
   - `rpg/systems/PROGRESSION_ECONOMY.md` — hero and companion growth, gear,
     the sources and sinks of every currency, the fairness model (what a free
     player has after 1, 10, 50 and 200 hours; what a paying player has), what
     is sold and why the world survives it, the anti-grind rules.
   - `rpg/systems/SESSION_UX.md` — the portrait screen described zone by zone,
     the thumb map, the three-minute session, the fifteen-minute session, the
     evening session, offline return, how the living world and the hero's
     standing are shown (rumours, notice boards, letters, a map that changes,
     how people greet the hero), accessibility.
   - `rpg/slice/FIRST_REGION.md` — the first region as a buildable proof: map
     in words, the home village and the town with twenty named people and
     schedules, three powers present and their opening moves, one season of
     world events, one dungeon with its reason and layers, the hero's first
     twenty hours traced from villager to a name people know, showing every
     system firing and at least six engine-generated quests in that trace.
3. **No claim without a trace.** A design claim ("factions act without the
   hero", "quests read as authored") is backed by a worked example in the
   document: state before, tick, state after, what the player sees.
4. **One designer writes.** One critic, a brutal AAA design director who
   writes no design, reads every file and judges by playing it on paper: three
   narrated sessions (three minutes, fifteen minutes, one evening) traced
   strictly from what the documents specify, plus the "friend test": the
   critic explains the world to an imagined friend using only the one page,
   and lists what the friend would misunderstand. Where a trace has to invent
   something the documents do not say, that is a hole and is listed.
5. The critic scores 0–10 per rubric line. 10 = the world reads as clear and
   weighty as Middle-earth, the engine's quests are indistinguishable from
   authored ones, and every system is specified to build; 8.5 = ship-ready
   design with nits; 7 = a good indie design doc; 5 = a feature list. Pass =
   every line ≥ 8.5 and no design-smell hit.
6. Below 8.5 the designer gets the ranked issue list with file paths and
   section names and goes again. Maximum 6 rounds. If still failing, stop and
   write down exactly what is missing and why.
7. Commit every round with the scores in the message. Keep
   `rpg/docs/STATUS.json` current with scores, open issues and word counts.
   Record every assumption in `rpg/docs/DECISIONS.md`. Critic reports go to
   `rpg/docs/critic/roundN.md` and `roundN.json`.

## Critic rubric

1. World: original, coherent, comprehensible; passes the friend test; the one
   page and the depth agree; a map with one shape; history that still matters
   in play.
2. Living world: all four minimums specified with inputs, state, tick,
   outputs and worked examples; the traces show the world changing without
   the hero and remembering the hero.
3. Adventure engine: the twelve sample quests read as authored, connect to
   the map and the lore, scale with standing, feed back into the simulation,
   and do not repeat each other in shape.
4. Classes and standing: three classes with distinct verbs in and out of
   combat; a subclass roadmap the base classes leave room for; the road from
   villager to force is earned, measured by the world and visible at every
   milestone.
5. Combat, dungeons and autobattle: readable one-thumb action; companions
   worth having; dungeons with reason, layers and cost; autobattle at parity;
   the six sample-encounter outcomes are credible and consistent.
6. Economy, session and UX: the fairness model holds at every hour mark;
   nothing sold buys power or time; the three session lengths each end with a
   world change; the portrait screen works one-handed; the living world and
   the hero's standing are visible on the phone.
7. Feasibility and honesty: content volumes and lore-table sizes counted; the
   first region buildable by a stated team size in a stated time; the engine's
   risks named; nothing promised that the documents do not specify.
8. Design-smell checklist: any hit is an automatic fail.

## Design smells (automatic fail)

A world that needs more than one page to explain, or more than four peoples,
five powers or three ages; gacha or energy under another name (timers, keys,
pulls, pity); anything sold that buys power or skips the world; a chosen one,
a prophecy, or a hero who starts with standing; factions that only react to
the hero; lore that never touches play; a generated quest that reads as a
template ("kill 10 wolves", "fetch the item"), names nobody, or connects to
nothing on the map; a class with no identity out of combat; a system with no
decision in it; an autobattle that is strictly better or strictly worse than
play; a feature listed without a session example; placeholder names
("Kingdom A", "Faction 2"); numbers without a model behind them; a screen that
needs two hands or landscape; any name, place, god, race or artefact
recognisable from Tolkien, Bethesda, Sir-Tech, Paizo or any other IP; "TBD"
anywhere in the bible.

## Rules

- Never inflate scores. Real numbers, failed rounds, what is still missing.
- Do not ask me questions. Decide, record assumptions in
  `rpg/docs/DECISIONS.md`, keep going.
- Do not touch anything outside `rpg/` in this repository. Do not depend on
  or modify bobnoe3000-maker/emberhold.

## Done means

`rpg/PITCH.md`, the world file, the six system files, `rpg/slice/FIRST_REGION.md`,
`rpg/docs/STATUS.json`, `rpg/docs/DECISIONS.md` and the critic reports exist.
Every rubric line scores ≥ 8.5 with no design-smell hit, or the report says
exactly which lines failed and why. Phase 2 (art direction, engine, first
playable) is a separate prompt and starts only after this passes.
